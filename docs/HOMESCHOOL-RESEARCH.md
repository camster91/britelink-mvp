# What makes a homeschool planning product genuinely useful

**Prepared for:** BriteLink  
**Research date:** 2026-09-19  
**Geographic emphasis:** Ontario, Canada, with Canadian, US, and England examples  
**Method:** Read-only review of public product pages, government rules, independent product reviews, and experienced-homeschooler guidance. No trials, accounts, forms, or outreach were used.

## Executive conclusion

The useful product is not the one with the most lessons. It is the one that turns family intent into a realistic **next action**, bends when the week changes, and quietly turns completed learning into defensible records. That conclusion is a synthesis of three evidence categories:

1. Experienced homeschool parents describe planning itself as tiring and anxiety-producing, especially when plans ignore work, health, life changes, and the child's interests ([TheHomeSchoolMom: simple planning](https://www.thehomeschoolmom.com/5-steps-to-simple-homeschool-planning-for-non-planners/)).
2. A mature planner's headline capabilities are automatic rescheduling, multi-student views, attendance/hours, grades, reports, and transcripts—not lesson content alone ([Homeschool Planet features](https://homeschoolplanet.com/features/)).
3. Legal obligations range from Ontario's low-regulation model to New York's plans, attendance hours, quarterly reports, and annual assessment, so records must be configurable by jurisdiction rather than hard-coded as one school model ([Ontario Education Act framework and PPM 131](https://www.ontario.ca/document/education-ontario-policy-and-program-direction/policyprogram-memorandum-131); [New York regulation 8 NYCRR 100.10](https://www.law.cornell.edu/regulations/new-york/8-NYCRR-100.10)).

**Best BriteLink opportunity:** preserve its strong personalized-plan and “Do this next” experience, then add a flexible family week and an automatic learning record. Do not turn it into a rigid school-at-home system.

## Ontario, Canada

### The practical answer first

Home schooling is legal in Ontario. The governing rule is Education Act s. 21(2)(a): a child is excused from compulsory school attendance when the child is receiving “satisfactory instruction at home or elsewhere.” The Ministry reproduces that statutory wording and explains the board process in PPM 131 ([Ontario PPM 131, Appendix A](https://www.ontario.ca/document/education-ontario-policy-and-program-direction/policyprogram-memorandum-131#section-6)). “Satisfactory instruction” is not defined in the Act or PPM 131, and the Ministry tells boards that home-school methods, materials, schedules, assessment, curriculum, and school year may differ from conventional school ([PPM 131 investigation guidance](https://www.ontario.ca/document/education-ontario-policy-and-program-direction/policyprogram-memorandum-131#section-3); [OFTP legal overview](https://ontariohomeschool.org/legal/)).

**Notification is policy guidance, not an express statutory filing duty.** PPM 131 says parents *should* notify the board in writing and *should* repeat that notice annually before September 1; a board should normally accept the notice as evidence of satisfactory instruction and not investigate ([PPM 131 procedures](https://www.ontario.ca/document/education-ontario-policy-and-program-direction/policyprogram-memorandum-131#section-2)). OFTP distinguishes that policy from the Education Act and states that the Act contains no legal requirement to submit a letter of intent, including for a child never enrolled in Ontario school ([OFTP notice guidance](https://ontariohomeschool.org/legal/letter-of-intent/); [OFTP on PPM 131's legal status](https://ontariohomeschool.org/legal/ppm-131/)). This distinction matters, but so does the practical caveat: PPM 131 lists refusal to notify as one possible reason for a board investigation, and a board acknowledgement letter is required for a homeschooled student to register directly with TVO ILC ([PPM 131](https://www.ontario.ca/document/education-ontario-policy-and-program-direction/policyprogram-memorandum-131#section-3); [TVO ILC FAQ](https://www.ilc.org/pages/faq)).

### What an Ontario family is legally required to keep or submit

For an independently home-schooled child, the sources reviewed identify **no routine legal requirement** to:

- keep or submit daily attendance or instructional-hour totals;
- file lesson plans, grades, report cards, portfolios, or work samples;
- follow the Ontario curriculum; or
- arrange standardized testing, board assessment, or annual evaluation.

That conclusion is bounded: it means these items are not routine statutory filings under the framework reviewed, not that records can never matter. PPM 131 says boards should normally not investigate after notice, but when there are reasonable grounds for concern, a board may ask about the education plan, literacy/numeracy, schedule, subjects, goals, curriculum, activities, work samples, materials, community resources, and assessment methods ([PPM 131](https://www.ontario.ca/document/education-ontario-policy-and-program-direction/policyprogram-memorandum-131#section-3)). EQAO assessments in Grades 3, 6, and 9 and the secondary literacy test are available to notified home-school families who request access; PPM 131 presents participation as optional ([PPM 131 access to assessments](https://www.ontario.ca/document/education-ontario-policy-and-program-direction/policyprogram-memorandum-131#section-4)). OFTP's FAQ likewise says testing is optional and no proof of homeschooling is required for ordinary grade placement when a child returns to school, although a principal may request an assessment for placement, especially in high school or where special needs are involved ([OFTP FAQ](https://ontariohomeschool.org/homeschool-faq/)).

### What is prudent to keep anyway

The following is a **practical record set, not a legal minimum**:

- dated list of subjects/topics, books/resources, activities, outings, tutors/co-ops, and approximate time where useful;
- a small sample of work or photos for each broad area, plus parent observations of progress;
- courses completed, provider, dates, course description, materials, assessment method, and final result;
- external evidence such as certificates, test results, volunteer/work hours, performances, publications, and recommendation contacts;
- copies of any withdrawal/notification and board acknowledgement letters.

These records provide ready evidence if a board has grounds to inquire under PPM 131, support a principal making a later placement or PLAR decision, and preserve options for admissions or employment. Ontario's PLAR policy says a student seeking a Grade 10–12 challenge must provide reasonable evidence of likely success and may use certificates or other records of accomplishment; the school principal, not the parent, conducts the formal evaluation and grants credit ([Ontario PPM 129](https://www.ontario.ca/document/education-ontario-policy-and-program-direction/policyprogram-memorandum-129)). For a non-OSSD route, OFTP shows portfolios, project evidence, course lists, and parent-created transcripts as tools families use to communicate learning, but these are not Ontario Student Transcripts and acceptance depends on the institution ([OFTP adulthood pathways](https://ontariohomeschool.org/unschooling-to-adulthood/); [sample application](https://ontariohomeschool.org/university/example1/)).

### Grades 9–12: where the records become consequential

Independent home schooling does **not itself issue Ontario credits, an Ontario Student Transcript (OST), or an Ontario Secondary School Diploma (OSSD)**. Ontario says only inspected private high schools and the public system may grant OSSD credits; not all private schools can do so ([Ontario private schools](https://www.ontario.ca/page/private-schools)). OFTP describes two distinct routes: remain an independent home learner and build evidence for the learner's chosen next step, or enrol in inspected/public credit providers so the school opens and maintains the Ontario Student Record and grants credits ([OFTP high-school guide](https://ontariohomeschool.org/highschool/)). TVO ILC is one such Ministry-recognized online school; it offers Ontario credits and the OSSD, and requires home-school applicants to provide the board's acknowledgement letter ([TVO ILC FAQ](https://www.ilc.org/pages/faq)).

For an OSSD, the province currently requires 30 credits, with the compulsory/optional split depending on the year Grade 9 began, plus literacy, online-learning, and 40-hour community-involvement requirements; newer cohorts also have a financial-literacy requirement ([Ontario graduation requirements](https://www.ontario.ca/page/earning-your-high-school-diploma)). If a home-schooled learner later enters an Ontario secondary school, PPM 131 says the relevant PLAR/equivalent-diploma provisions apply; PPM 129 explains that the principal controls formal evaluation and credit granting ([PPM 131, PLAR](https://www.ontario.ca/document/education-ontario-policy-and-program-direction/policyprogram-memorandum-131#section-5); [PPM 129](https://www.ontario.ca/document/education-ontario-policy-and-program-direction/policyprogram-memorandum-129)). Families should therefore keep substantially better records from Grade 9 onward, but should not label parent-awarded marks or courses as official Ontario credits.

University admission is institution-specific. For example, Carleton currently asks home-schooled applicants for official evidence through six Grade 12 U/M courses from a Ministry-approved provider, recognized credentials such as AP/A-levels, or—in the absence of those grades—SAT/ACT results, plus biographical information; prerequisite and scholarship consequences vary by route ([Carleton home-schooled admissions](https://admissions.carleton.ca/applicant-type/home-schooled-applicants/)). This is why BriteLink should store target-program prerequisites and official-provider credits separately from a family-created learning transcript.

### What Ontario parents appear to worry about

No representative Ontario survey ranking parent concerns was found, so “most” cannot be stated statistically. The strongest public indicators are the questions Ontario's support association repeatedly addresses:

- **Legality and board contact:** whether notice is mandatory, what information a board may request, and how to respond to overreach ([OFTP notice guidance](https://ontariohomeschool.org/legal/letter-of-intent/); [OFTP legal overview](https://ontariohomeschool.org/legal/)).
- **Whether the child is learning enough:** an OFTP article is devoted to how parents know learning is occurring without formal testing, showing the tension between direct observation and school-style measurement ([OFTP](https://ontariohomeschool.org/oct01hrselection/)).
- **High-school capability and future options:** OFTP explicitly organizes its high-school guidance around parent teaching confidence, the OSSD, credits, post-secondary admission, and employment ([OFTP high-school guide](https://ontariohomeschool.org/highschool/)).
- **Teen wellbeing and fit:** its teen-transition guidance starts with academic struggle, bullying/discrimination, stress, anxiety/depression, medical absence, gifted boredom, and the need to accommodate arts or athletics ([OFTP teen guide](https://ontariohomeschool.org/switching-to-home-based-learning-in-the-teen-years-in-ontario/)).

**Ontario product implication:** default to a low-burden learning log and “peace-of-mind” summary, not compulsory attendance theatre. Make an optional PPM 131 evidence pack, keep notification and acknowledgement documents, and add a separate Grade 9–12 pathway planner that clearly distinguishes independent learning evidence from official OSSD credits and transcripts.

## 1. Top real problems, ranked for product priority

This ordering is **not a prevalence ranking**. No source found measured all seven problems in one representative Ontario sample. It is a product-priority ranking based on how directly each problem affects the weekly parent workflow, whether it recurs across parent guidance/product design/review evidence, and the consequence if BriteLink does not solve it. Each item identifies the evidence supporting it; the ranking itself is an analyst synthesis.

### 1. Converting good intentions into a workable week without planning exhaustion

An experienced parent describes buying five planners, taking planning courses, spending “countless hours” planning and feeling tired before teaching began. The same source says a usable plan must account for the parent's work, health, moves, schedule, the children's strengths and interests, and inevitable illness or disruption ([source](https://www.thehomeschoolmom.com/5-steps-to-simple-homeschool-planning-for-non-planners/)).

**Product implication:** generate a small, editable week from goals and constraints; show one next action; avoid requiring the parent to construct a complete calendar before receiving value.

### 2. Recovering when real life breaks the plan

The parent planning account cited above says a usable plan must survive sickness, work changes, moves, and other disruptions rather than assume every day will go as planned ([parent source](https://www.thehomeschoolmom.com/5-steps-to-simple-homeschool-planning-for-non-planners/)). Homeschool Planet's automatic rescheduling and Time4Learning's schedule customization show how current vendors respond to that need; they are product evidence, not proof of prevalence ([Homeschool Planet](https://homeschoolplanet.com/features/); [Time4Learning](https://www.time4learning.com/)).

**Product implication:** “move one lesson” is not enough. Parents need “skip today and rebalance the week,” “carry unfinished work,” “protect appointments,” and “pause this subject until Monday.”

### 3. Knowing whether the children are learning “enough” without recreating school

Experienced-homeschool guidance explicitly addresses parents' anxiety about whether learning is enough, recommends long-term and yearly goals, and warns against comparison with other children or families ([source](https://www.thehomeschoolmom.com/homeschooling-how-do-i-know-if-im-doing-enough/)). A burnout account links comparison, excessive materials, timetable pressure, late-night marking, and too many activities to parent anxiety and burnout ([source](https://www.thehomeschoolmom.com/how-to-avoid-homeschool-burnout/)).

**Product implication:** provide calm evidence of progress—goals touched, subjects encountered, work completed, observations, and recent wins—without declaring that seat time or grade-level pacing is the only valid measure.

### 4. Teaching several children at different ages without multiplying preparation

Separate grade-level lessons for every child create many lessons to prepare. Multi-age, cross-subject activities let a family teach together while varying expectations by child ([source](https://www.thehomeschoolmom.com/homeschooling-multiple-ages-multiple-subjects/)). Homeschool Planet accordingly supports viewing any combination of students in one calendar ([source](https://homeschoolplanet.com/features/)).

**Product implication:** add a whole-family view and shared activities with child-specific outcomes, rather than only a learner switcher.

### 5. Keeping the curriculum fitted to the child, not forcing the child through the curriculum

Homeschool parents report poor fit when a curriculum conflicts with a child's level, learning needs, interests, the family's schedule, energy, budget, or screen-time preferences. Children may be at different levels in different subjects, and continuing an expensive curriculum solely because it was purchased is specifically discouraged ([source](https://www.thehomeschoolmom.com/october-1-curriculums-not-working-day/)). IXL provides a concrete product example: Common Sense Media's **AI-generated summary of 1,644 parent reviews** says users frequently criticize repetitive exercises, SmartScore penalties, frustration, and demotivation, while its editorial review praises detailed practice and diagnostic information ([source](https://www.commonsensemedia.org/website-reviews/ixl)).

**Product implication:** support level-by-subject, easy substitutions, interest-led alternatives, offline choices, and an explicit “this is not working” path that adapts the plan without treating the family as behind.

### 6. Producing records and reports without doing the work twice

The burden is highly jurisdiction-dependent. Florida requires a contemporaneous activity/reading log, work samples, two-year portfolio retention, and an annual evaluation ([statute](https://www.leg.state.fl.us/statutes/index.cfm?App_mode=Display_Statute&URL=1000-1099/1002/Sections/1002.41.html)). New York requires an individual plan, attendance records, 900 or 990 annual instructional hours depending on grade, quarterly subject/progress reports, and an annual assessment ([regulation](https://www.law.cornell.edu/regulations/new-york/8-NYCRR-100.10)). Homeschool Planet's record features—attendance, hours, grades, transcripts, course descriptions, and assignment reports—show how a planner can turn daily use into reports ([source](https://homeschoolplanet.com/features/)).

**Product implication:** every completion, note, photo, reading, outing, and assessment should optionally become a dated learning-log entry. Reports should be generated from that activity, not entered again.

### 7. Sustaining parent capacity and child interest over time

Experienced-homeschool guidance frames a parent no longer wanting to continue, believing school is now better for the child, a planned high-school transition, and changed family, economic, or relationship circumstances as legitimate reasons to stop; it does **not** measure how often those reasons occur ([source](https://www.thehomeschoolmom.com/good-reasons-quit-homeschooling/)). Separate guidance describes curriculum mismatch as a source of child unhappiness and parent frustration ([source](https://www.thehomeschoolmom.com/october-1-curriculums-not-working-day/)).

**Product implication:** retention should come from reducing load and improving fit, not from rigid streaks. Ask lightweight check-ins such as “Is this pace still working?” and make pausing, simplifying, or changing approach safe.

### What the comparison says

The market splits into three incomplete categories:

- **Content systems** (Time4Learning, Khan, IXL, Sonlight, The Good and the Beautiful, Teach-nology) reduce lesson sourcing but usually do not manage the whole family's mixed online/offline learning record.
- **Administrative planners** (especially Homeschool Planet) manage time and records well but rely on parents or paid add-ons for much of the learning plan.
- **BriteLink's current service** supplies a personalized, educator-authored plan and support, but lacks the administrative record and whole-family flexibility that make a planner useful every day.

That gap is a credible product position: **personalized human-reviewed planning + flexible daily execution + automatic jurisdiction-aware records**.

## 3. Record-keeping and reporting worth supporting

### Ontario: low routine burden; records preserve options

As detailed in [Ontario, Canada](#ontario-canada), the Education Act does not expressly require a notice filing, attendance log, portfolio, annual report, or assessment from an independently home-schooling family. PPM 131 nevertheless recommends annual written notice and permits a board with reasonable grounds for concern to ask for an education plan and evidence ([Ontario PPM 131](https://www.ontario.ca/document/education-ontario-policy-and-program-direction/policyprogram-memorandum-131); [OFTP notice guidance](https://ontariohomeschool.org/legal/letter-of-intent/)). Records become materially important in Grades 9–12 because only authorized schools grant OSSD credits and maintain official Ontario records, while prior-learning recognition and post-secondary alternatives require evidence ([Ontario private schools](https://www.ontario.ca/page/private-schools); [Ontario PPM 129](https://www.ontario.ca/document/education-ontario-policy-and-program-direction/policyprogram-memorandum-129); [Carleton admissions](https://admissions.carleton.ca/applicant-type/home-schooled-applicants/)).

**Support in BriteLink:** optional notice/acknowledgement storage; one-click PPM 131 evidence pack; low-friction learning log and work samples; and a Grade 9–12 pathway that keeps parent records visibly separate from official OSSD credits, OSR/OST records, and provider-issued transcripts.

### Alberta: program plan and portfolio matter in supervised programs

Parents in both supervised and notification-only home education are responsible for planning, managing, providing, evaluating, and supervising study. Supervised programs receive at least two formal evaluations each year, including portfolio review; notification-only families notify annually but do not submit the program plan to the Minister ([Alberta government](https://www.alberta.ca/home-education)).

**Support in BriteLink:** annual notice reminder, outcome-based program plan, portfolio selection, evaluation dates, evaluator notes, and reimbursement-receipt attachments where relevant.

### British Columbia: annual registration, parent-owned program

B.C. requires annual registration by September 30. Homeschool programs are not required to meet provincial standards and are not supervised or inspected by the Ministry; registering schools offer evaluation/assessment and loan resources, which families may accept or reject ([B.C. government](https://www2.gov.bc.ca/gov/content/education-training/k-12/support/classroom-alternatives/homeschooling)).

**Support in BriteLink:** registration reminder/receipt, parent-defined program and goals, optional assessment records, and borrowed-resource tracking.

### Florida: contemporaneous portfolio plus annual evaluation

Florida requires a notice of intent, a portfolio containing a contemporaneous educational-activity log with reading titles and samples of work, two-year portfolio retention, and an annual evaluation filed with the district ([Florida Statutes §1002.41](https://www.leg.state.fl.us/statutes/index.cfm?App_mode=Display_Statute&URL=1000-1099/1002/Sections/1002.41.html)).

**Support in BriteLink:** dated learning log, reading list, photo/file evidence, two-year retention controls, evaluator workflow, and annual export.

### New York: the strongest example of recurring administrative burden

New York requires annual notice, an Individualized Home Instruction Plan (IHIP), required subjects, 180 days, 900 annual hours for grades 1–6 or 990 for grades 7–12, parent-maintained attendance, quarterly reports with hours/material/grade-or-narrative, and an annual assessment ([8 NYCRR 100.10](https://www.law.cornell.edu/regulations/new-york/8-NYCRR-100.10)).

**Support in BriteLink:** IHIP fields, jurisdiction-specific subject checklist, day/hour totals, quarterly report generation, coverage-vs-plan explanations, grade or narrative choice, assessment record, and deadlines.

### England: suitable full-time education without a mandatory national curriculum

Parents must ensure full-time education from age five but need not follow the national curriculum; a council can make an informal enquiry and use a school attendance order if it believes suitable education is not being provided ([GOV.UK](https://www.gov.uk/home-education)).

**Support in BriteLink:** parent-defined aims, suitable-education narrative, broad progress/evidence export, and council-contact log—without forcing national-curriculum mapping.

### Common data model

One neutral learning record can serve all of these contexts:

- learner, jurisdiction, school year, and reporting mode;
- dated learning event, subject(s), activity type, duration (optional), location, and participants;
- goal/outcome links chosen by the parent;
- resource or reading title;
- status plus parent observation or assessment narrative;
- evidence attachments or links;
- attendance/day flag where legally relevant;
- evaluator and submission records;
- retention rule and export history.

The interface should reveal only the fields required by the selected jurisdiction and family preference. Legal templates need a “verify current local requirements” notice because rules change and district practice can vary.

## 4. The spectrum of home-education styles

There is no single homeschool workflow. A broad overview identifies school-at-home, online school, unit studies, classical, Montessori, Waldorf, Charlotte Mason, relaxed/eclectic, outsourcing, and unschooling, and notes that experienced families often combine or change methods ([source](https://www.thehomeschoolmom.com/homeschooling-styles/)). Ontario explicitly tells boards that homeschool methodology, materials, schedules, assessment, curriculum, and school day/year may differ from conventional schools ([Ontario PPM 131](https://www.ontario.ca/document/education-ontario-policy-and-program-direction/policyprogram-memorandum-131)).

| Style | What the family may want | Product behaviour that respects it |
|---|---|---|
| **Structured / school-at-home** | Subjects, sequence, timetable, assignments, grades | Detailed calendar, prerequisites, grading, attendance/hours, curriculum mapping |
| **Online / outsourced mix** | Assignments from several providers and tutors | Links/imports, provider field, consolidated calendar and progress without pretending BriteLink delivered the course |
| **Classical** | Grammar → logic → rhetoric progression; language-rich study | Long arcs, reading/discussion/memory/work products, stage labels rather than only grade labels ([style source](https://www.thehomeschoolmom.com/homeschooling-styles/)) |
| **Charlotte Mason** | Living books, short lessons, narration, habits, art/music, nature | Reading log, narration note/audio, habit and nature-study entries, short flexible blocks ([style source](https://www.thehomeschoolmom.com/homeschooling-styles/)) |
| **Unit study / project-based** | One theme across several subjects and children | One family activity linked to many subjects, goals, children, and differentiated outcomes ([multi-age source](https://www.thehomeschoolmom.com/homeschooling-multiple-ages-multiple-subjects/)) |
| **Eclectic / relaxed** | Mix-and-match resources; uneven subject cadence | No mandatory daily subject grid; easy add/replace/pause; level-by-subject; flexible weekly targets |
| **Unschooling / interest-led** | Child-set goals; learning through life, projects, play, places, and people | Capture-first learning log, emergent tags, observations and artifacts; no assumption that learning begins as a scheduled lesson ([unschooling source](https://www.thehomeschoolmom.com/the-beginners-guide-to-unschooling/)) |
| **Multi-age family learning** | Shared read-alouds, outings, themes, experiments | One event assigned to several children with a different note/outcome for each ([source](https://www.thehomeschoolmom.com/homeschooling-multiple-ages-multiple-subjects/)) |

**Design rule:** ask for the family's desired degree of structure (for example, “plan every day,” “weekly goals,” or “capture learning after it happens”), not for a permanent philosophical label.

## 5. What may make parents stay, change products, or quit

Public evidence on actual renewal and churn is weak: the fetched vendors do not publish retention data, and no representative parent-exit survey was found. The defensible signals are:

- **Lower workload and flexible recovery are plausible retention features, not proven retention rates.** Time4Learning and Homeschool Planet foreground automatic grading, stored progress, planning, reusable reports, self-paced work, schedule control, and rescheduling; this proves what the products offer and market, not that any one feature causes renewal ([Time4Learning](https://www.time4learning.com/products/fifth-grade-curriculum); [Homeschool Planet](https://homeschoolplanet.com/features/)).
- **Curriculum mismatch is direct switching guidance.** An experienced homeschool resource recommends changing curriculum when it conflicts with the child's level, needs, interests, family schedule, energy, supplies, budget, or screen preferences ([source](https://www.thehomeschoolmom.com/october-1-curriculums-not-working-day/)).
- **Child frustration is visible in review evidence.** Common Sense Media's AI-generated summary of 1,644 IXL parent reviews links repetition and SmartScore drops with frustration, anxiety, and demotivation; this supports dissatisfaction, but the page does not report cancellation rates ([source](https://www.commonsensemedia.org/website-reviews/ixl)).
- **Ending homeschooling is sometimes a family-level decision, not product churn.** An experienced homeschool author identifies parent willingness, the child's needs, a planned high-school transition, and changed family/economic/relationship circumstances as legitimate reasons to stop. This is normative guidance, not observed prevalence ([source](https://www.thehomeschoolmom.com/good-reasons-quit-homeschooling/)).
- **Price changes the cost of trying or switching, but its effect was not measured.** Free downloads and low-cost tools reduce commitment, while Sonlight's complete packages exceed $1,000 and Homeschool Planet charges separately for optional lesson plans ([The Good and the Beautiful](https://www.goodandbeautiful.com/pages/free); [Sonlight catalog](https://www.sonlight.com/collections/all/products.json?limit=250); [Homeschool Planet](https://homeschoolplanet.com/features/)).

**BriteLink implication:** track real cancellation reasons, plan-adaptation events, repeated skips, parent effort, child-frustration signals, and cohort retention. Until that evidence exists, treat low workload, flexibility, fit, child experience, and price as testable retention hypotheses rather than settled causes.

## 8. Recommended product shape

### The smallest genuinely useful daily loop

1. **Open Today:** see 1–5 recommended activities across the family, with time, help, materials, and offline/online labels.
2. **Adapt:** complete, pause, skip, swap, or rebalance without losing work.
3. **Capture:** one tap records completion; optionally add time, observation, photo/file, reading, or assessment.
4. **See progress:** a weekly narrative and simple coverage view use the family's goals, not an assumed classroom timetable.
5. **Export when needed:** Ontario plan/evidence summary, Alberta portfolio, Florida portfolio/evaluation packet, or New York IHIP/quarterly report are generated from the same underlying record.

### Product principles

- **Planning should save more time than it consumes.** Default to a usable suggestion; let detail be optional.
- **Plans are hypotheses, not contracts.** Preserve history, but make adaptation normal.
- **Capture learning that happened, not only assignments BriteLink prescribed.**
- **One family, many learners.** Share activities and separate outcomes.
- **Progress is plural.** Support narrative, mastery, project artifacts, hours, grades, or coverage according to family and jurisdiction.
- **Jurisdiction-aware, not legal-advice theatre.** Cite the official rule, show last-checked date, expose only relevant fields, and tell families to verify current local requirements.
- **Do not use anxiety as retention.** No shame-based streaks, “behind” labels, or universal school pacing.
